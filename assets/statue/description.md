Figure of the Bearer, Carved hardwood, origin uncertain · c. 19th century

This solitary figure stands with one arm raised, balancing a great vessel upon the crown of the head—a posture the unknown carver has frozen mid-stride, as though the bearer were caught eternally on some unfinished journey. The deeply incised grooves across the torso and skirt were likely cut with a single curved blade, the marks left intentionally visible, lending the surface a restless, woven texture. The serene, slightly downturned face suggests neither burden nor strain, but quiet acceptance of an endless task.

Local tradition holds that such figures were custodial rather than decorative: placed at the threshold of a dwelling, the Bearer was said to carry away the household's misfortune at each day's end, returning empty by dawn. The vessel itself is hollowed but bears no residue—some scholars propose it once held grain or water in ritual offering, others that it was meant always to remain empty, a promise rather than a provision. Acquired by the museum in 1931 through an anonymous bequest, the figure arrived with no documentation save a single word, untranslated, inked upon its base.
